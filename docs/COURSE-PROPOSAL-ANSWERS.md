## 1. Why is there a perceived need for this course?

### Option A: employer signal (closest to the dean's framing)

> AI tools now let people with no programming background build working software, and employers increasingly expect new MBA hires to use them well. Saying "I used ChatGPT" no longer sets a candidate apart. What does is having built something that addresses a real business problem, and being able to explain how it was scoped, tested, and where it still falls short. In six weeks, this course gives every student a working application and the experience to discuss it credibly with recruiters and managers.

### Option B: managerial skill (built to last)

> Agentic AI has moved the bottleneck in software development from writing code to deciding what to build, directing the work, and judging whether the result is correct. Those are managerial skills: scoping, specifying, prioritizing, and evaluating. MBA students are well suited to them but rarely practice them end to end on a real product. This course teaches that loop through a structured process that is meant to outlast any particular AI tool.

### Evidence for "Employers increasingly look for…" (searched 2026-10-13)

**Primary source: [GMAC 2026 Corporate Recruiters Survey](https://www.gmac.com/-/media/files/gmac/research/employment-outlook/2026-corporate-recruiters-survey/report.pdf)** (621 recruiters and hiring managers, 39 countries, fielded January–May 2026, released June 25, 2026). Page numbers are from the report.

- **Rising demand.** The share of employers naming "skills in using AI tools" among the most important skills for current graduates rose from **31% (2025) to 50% (2026)**. That was the largest year-over-year increase of any skill measured (p. 8–9).
- **Top future skill.** Asked which skills will matter most in five years, employers ranked AI-tool skills **first (53%)**, for the second year in a row (p. 11).
- **Preparation gap.** Only **49%** of employers rated graduates very well or adequately prepared in AI-tool skills (16% + 33%), one of only four skills below half (p. 10). GMAC calls this "an urgent curricular gap for programs to continue to address" (p. 7).
- **Judgment matters more than tool skill.** GMAC writes that more important than AI-tool skills themselves "are the ability to analyze the output, adapt based on the new information, and effectively communicate a new approach" (p. 8). Its conclusion makes the same point: employers aren't looking for graduates who can use AI tools in isolation. This maps directly onto "evaluate and champion."
- **What employers want AI used for.** Among employers who prioritized AI skills, **67%** want graduates to use AI to automate tasks, and just under half (48%) want them to use it to develop strategy and make decisions (p. 17).
- **Entry-level pressure.** One in three employers has already replaced some entry-level roles with AI (p. 6).

**Broader labor market (not MBA-specific):**

- [PwC 2026 Global AI Jobs Barometer](https://www.aap.com.au/aapreleases/cision20260615ae78182/) (June 15, 2026; 1B+ job ads, 27 countries): jobs requiring AI skills carry a **62% wage premium**, up from 57%, and are growing **69% vs. 9%** for all jobs.
- [Lightcast, "Beyond the Buzz"](https://lightcast.io/resources/blog/beyond-the-buzz-press-release-2025-07-23) (July 2025; 1.3B postings): **51%** of postings requiring AI skills are outside IT and computer science, and those postings advertise **28% (~$18K)** higher salaries.
**Demonstrated experience (the "proof" point):**

- [ZipRecruiter 2026 AI Employer Report](https://www.ziprecruiter-research.org/economic-insights-research/ai-employer-report-2026) (1,000+ verified talent-acquisition professionals and hiring managers, surveyed June 11–18, 2026): **50%** of employers expect candidates to already be practical or advanced AI users, and **74%** see AI skills as a strong advantage or a requirement for at least some roles. This is the best fit for "demonstrated," but it's a vendor survey and isn't specific to MBAs.
- [ZipRecruiter Q2 2026 New Hires Survey](https://www.ziprecruiter-research.org/new-hires-survey-2026-q2) (1,500+ recent hires): **32%** were tested on AI proficiency during hiring, and **11%** had to show it in an interview or skills assessment. This is the worker-side view of the same trend.
- [NACE Job Outlook 2026 Spring Update](https://naceweb.org/about-us/press/2026/ready-or-reluctant-employer-expectations-for-ai-skills-meet-student-skepticism) (185 employers, February–March 2026): more than a third of entry-level jobs require AI skills, nearly triple the share six months earlier, and nearly 60% of employers give interns projects that use AI tools. It's credible but undergraduate-focused, and only 28% of employers say they want early-career hires who can use AI, which works against the claim.
- **Avoid:** the [CSP Global MBA-hiring questionnaire](https://online.csp.edu/resources/article/more-employers-plan-to-hire-mbas-in-2026-and-ai-fluency-is-now-the-baseline/), which reports AI fluency as the top priority (35%) and a "portfolio of applied work" (25%). It's the only MBA-specific "portfolio" figure I found, but the university that sells the program commissioned it and calls it non-scientific.
- [Microsoft/LinkedIn 2024 Work Trend Index](https://news.microsoft.com/source/?p=19728): **66%** of leaders said they wouldn't hire someone without AI skills. This is the most quoted figure, but it's two years old and measures stated intent, so use it only as a backup.

**Counterpoint a dean may raise.** Even after the jump, AI-tool skills ranked only **14th** among current skills (up from 16th in 2025). GMAC calls AI "an increasingly valued but still supportive capability rather than the defining skill employers seek" (p. 8). This supports "increasingly" but not "top priority." It also helps your case: the course teaches what GMAC says matters more, which is analyzing output and communicating decisions, not tool use for its own sake.

**What the evidence doesn't cover.** No source measures demand for MBAs who *build software* with AI. The closest support is "using AI to automate tasks" (67%) and the emphasis on analyzing AI output. Keep the claim at the level of "frame, direct, evaluate, champion" rather than "build apps."

### Supporting points to draw on

- The dean's outline already states the core of the need: interviewers want to hear more than "I used ChatGPT," and the skills that last are specifying, decomposing, verifying, and judgment, because the tools will change within a year. Echoing the deans' own framing back to them is low-risk.
- No prior programming is required, so the course reaches students outside the tech track, which is the group that otherwise has the least access to this skill.
- Demo Day (week 6) can double as an employer-relations event. The dean's outline suggested inviting recruiters, alumni, and Career Management Center coaches.
- **[Fill in if available]** Local evidence of demand, such as student requests, club interest, CMC or recruiter feedback, or waitlists for related electives. GMAC covers the employer side (see above); a Fuqua-specific data point would cover the student side.
- **[Confirm before claiming]** That no existing Fuqua course has students build and deploy software with AI. I don't know the catalog, so I left this out of the drafts.

### Weak spots

- **~~The need statement is pure assertion.~~** GMAC 2026 now supports "increasingly" (see the evidence section). It doesn't support claiming AI is employers' *top* priority, and nothing measures demand for MBAs who build software specifically.
- **There's a tension between teams and individual portfolios.** The dean's outline chose individual projects specifically so each student could honestly say "I built this," and your outline uses team projects. If the need statement leans on interview portfolios (Options A and C), expect someone to ask about it. A prepared answer: weeks 1–2 produce individual artifacts (the resume site and the project plan), the week 4 product review is individual, and self- and peer-assessments document each person's contribution. Consider adding a sentence saying so, or moving to Option B, which doesn't depend on individual portfolios.
- **Don't name vendors in the need statement.** Your schedule centers ChatGPT Work. Tying the *justification* to a product invites "what happens when the tool changes?"
